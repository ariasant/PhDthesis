\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 1.0  Opening preamble (before Section 1.1)
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------


The $\Lambda$CDM model describes a universe composed of mostly dark energy ($\sim 70\%$), which drives the accelerated expansion
of the universe at late times, cold dark matter ($\sim 25\%$), which is a collisionless type of matter that does not interact 
electromagnetically, and the remaining $\sim 5\%$ being baryonic matter described by the standard model of particle physics 
\citep{PlanckCollaboration2016}. According to this paradigm, galaxies form within dark matter halos originating 
from the non-linear growth of gravitational instability of primordial adiabatic density fluctuations \citep{Peebles1965,Silk1968}.
In the early Universe these fluctuations are of small amplitude compared to the surrounding density background, 
and they grow linearly with the expansion of the universe as denser regions attract more matter, while cosmic voids become more underdense. 
Once the matter density of region becomes comparable to the surrounding, it decouples from the overall expansion, ``turns around'' 
and collapses into a virialised, self-gravitating structure called a halo \citep{Press1974}. 
 
As dark matter dominates over baryonic matter, the non-linear regime of density perturbations leads to the formation 
of galaxies as described by a two-stage theory formulated by \citet{White1978}.
First, the dominant collisionless cold dark matter relaxes violently into quasi-equilibrium, self-gravitating dark
matter haloes; then, the baryonic gas that collapses alongside the dark matter is shock-heated to the virial temperature
of its host halo. Gas at this temperature subsequently cools through radiative processes, losing pressure support and
condensing towards the centre of the gravitational potential of the halo, where it can fragment and form stars
giving rise to a luminous galaxy.

Because the primordial fluctuations have larger amplitudes on smaller mass scales, low-mass haloes are the first to collapse 
and subsequently coalesce into ever larger systems \cite{Smith2003}. Structure grows hierarchically, in a ``bottom-up'' fashion, 
with massive haloes such as that of the Milky Way assembled through the merging and accretion 
of a multitude of smaller progenitors \citep{White1978,Searle1978}. Because of the massive ($m \sim  \text{GeV}$) nature of the ``cold'' dark matter particle, 
matter can cluster and virialise forming halos at scale as small as the Earth mass \cite{Diemand2005}. 
Thus, the cold dark matter model predicts the presence of thousands of subhalos orbiting around a Milky Way-like galaxy \cite{Springel2008}.

However, only a fraction of that are observed as luminous satellites \cite{McConnachie2012}. While
this ``missing satellites'' problem can be alleviated by appealing to baryonic feedback mechanisms which can devoid 
smaller halos of stars, it also motivates the exploration of alternative cosmological models as solutions to this
and other small-scale challenges of the $\Lambda\text{CDM}$ paradigm \cite{Bullock2017}.
The number and characteristics of these merger events strictly relates to the nature assumed 
for the dark matter particle. For instance, in warm dark matter ($m \sim  \text{keV}$) cosmologies, 
the free-streaming scale of the particles is expected to suppress matter overdensities with mass $<10^{7} \, M_{\odot}$ \cite{Bode2001}, 
hence bringing the mass limit of collapsed structures closer to the one needed to ignite star formation \cite{Jethwa2018}. 
Similarly, in the fuzzy dark matter regime ($m \sim  10^{-22} \,\text{eV}$) \cite{Hu2000}, 
the wave properties due to the quantum nature of the particles forbid the formation of halos 
less massive than $10^{7} \, M_{\odot}$. 

Therefore, reconstructing the number and properties of the events 
in a galaxy hierarchical formation is an important test for the standard cosmological model and a
probe of the viability of alternative paradigms.
In the remainder of this chapter I describe techniques for how the inference of a galaxy's assembly history
from its present-day stellar content (Section~\ref{sec:intro_lcdm}). Then, I proceed to review the results of
applying these techniques to the Milky Way (Section~\ref{sec:intro_mwhistory}). I conclude the chapter by
introducing cosmological simulations as test beds for these techniques and reviewing the state-of-the-art
machine learning methods built upon them (Section~\ref{sec:intro_ml}), before laying out the structure of
the thesis (Section~\ref{sec:intro_outline}).

\section{Stellar probes of past mergers}
\label{sec:intro_lcdm}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------


A natural consequence of the hierarchical formation of galaxies is the formation of an extended, diffuse stellar halo,
built largely from the stars tidally stripped (i.e.\ \textit{accreted}) from disrupted satellite galaxies \citep{Bullock2005,Cooper2010}. Because stellar populations retain
a memory of the environment in which they formed \citep{Eggen1962,Searle1978}, the halo is a trove of fossil
information about a galaxy's formation history \citep{Helmi2020,Deason2024}, effectively connecting a galaxy's present structure to its turbulent past.
Despite comprising only $\sim 1\%$ of the total stellar mass (e.g. $M_{\rm halo} \sim 10^9\,M_\odot$ for the Milky Way; 
\citealt{Deason2019,Mackereth2020}), the stellar halo encodes the full record of hierarchical assembly.
During the process of accretion, stars remain spatially coherent as tidal streams. However, as the disrupted system performs 
multiple revolutions around the host, the debris lose their coherence, phase-mixing with the stars 
born \textit{in-situ} \citep{Tremaine1999,Binney2008}. 

The signatures of past mergers are imprinted in the kinematic, chemical, and photometric properties of accreted stars. 
From the information retained in this space one can, in principle, infer the number of merging
events comprising the assembly history of a galaxy as well as properties of the disrupted systems such as their masses, 
star formation histories, and orbital properties \citep{Freeman2002}. 

\subsection{Chemical tagging}

Each satellite galaxy undergoes its own, independent chemical evolution, governed by its mass, 
star formation history and the efficiency with which it retains and recycles the metals produced by successive generations 
of stars. The stars formed within a given progenitor therefore inherit a characteristic pattern of elemental abundances, i.e.
a chemical ``fingerprint'', which is fixed at birth and is unaffected by the subsequent dynamical mixing of the debris. 
Through the principle of \textit{chemical tagging} \citep{Freeman2002}, stars sharing such a fingerprint can be grouped 
in the systems where they formed even when they have become widely dispersed in phase space. 

Chemical abundances are quantified with the standard bracket notation

\begin{equation}
    [\mathrm{X/Y}] \equiv \log_{10}(N_X/N_Y)_\star - \log_{10}(N_X/N_Y)_\odot,
\end{equation}

where $N_X$ and $N_Y$ are number densities of elements X and Y and the subscript $\odot$ denotes solar values,
so that $[\mathrm{X/Y}]=0$ by definition for the Sun.
Two abundance ratios widely used are the iron abundance $[\mathrm{Fe/H}]$, which measures the
overall degree of chemical enrichment of the gas from which a star formed, and the $\alpha$-element-to-iron
ratio $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$, which can be interpreted as a ``chemical clock'' of the star formation history of the
progenitor \citep{Font2006a}. The production of $\alpha$-elements dominates the early phases of a galaxy chemical evolution as
core-collapse supernovae \citep{Portinari1998,Kobayashi2006}, their main production channel, occur on timescales
of $\sim 3\text{--}30$ Myr due to the high mass ($M_\star \gtrsim 8 M_\odot$, \citep{Portinari1998}) of their stellar progenitors. 
Iron  elements are, instead, contributed predominantly by Type~Ia supernovae \citep{Iwamoto1999}, whose
typical timescale for enrichment in a Milky Way-like galaxy is $\sim 1$ Gyr \citep{Matteucci2009}. 

Therefore, during the chemical evolution of the progenitor, its $[\mathrm{Fe/H}]$
increases as the gas becomes more enriched, while its $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ decreases as the contribution of
Type~Ia supernovae becomes more significant. For this reason, satellite galaxies disrupted by early mergers, end their chemical evolution
before the onset of Type~Ia supernovae, and their stars are expected to have higher $[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ at lower $[\mathrm{Fe/H}]$
than those accreted at later times \citep{Font2006a}. Hence, the measurements of the chemical abundaces of accreted stars not only can
be used for grouping them into the systems where they formed, but also to infer the epoch of accretion of their progenitors.


\subsection{Clustering in integrals-of-motion space}

Galaxies can be modelled, to an excellent approximation, as collisionless systems.
Thus, their phase-space distribution function $f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v},t)$, i.e.\ the probability density of finding
a star at a given position $\mathbf{r}$ and velocity $\mathbf{v}$, is conserved along stellar orbits, as described by the collisionless
Boltzmann equation \citep{Binney2008},
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
    + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{r}} f
    - \nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\Phi \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{v}} f = 0,
\label{eq:cbe}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(\mathbf{r})$ is the gravitational potential.
Therefore, as newly accreted debris evolve in a gravitational potential
losing their coherence in configuration space, they remain clustered in phase space. Following Hamiltonian dynamics, the
orbits of these stars can be represented through a new set of action-angle coordinates
where all the momenta are constants. These new quantities conserved in time are also called \textit{integrals of motion}.
Thus, stars accreted from the same satellite that follow similar orbits are described by similar actions (integrals of motion),
and they remain clustered in this space even after they have dispersed in physical space.

For a galaxy with a spherically symmetric gravitational potential, the integrals of motion are the total orbital energy,
$E = \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{v}|^2 + \Phi(\mathbf{r})$,
and the three components of the angular momentum vector $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}$.
In the axisymmetric potential of a disc galaxy, only the $z$-component
is strictly conserved alongside $E$; so that the two-dimensional $(E,\,L_z)$ space is often used to identify 
accreted substructure \citep{Helmi1999a,Koppelman2018,Myeong2019,Lovdal2022,Ou2023,Dodd2023}.
However, as the geometry of the matter distribution changes and the potential becomes more complex,
so does the computation of the integrals of motion. Moreover, as a galaxy builds up its mass through mergers and star formation,
its gravitational potential evolves in time, and the integrals of motion are no longer strictly conserved.
The efficacy of integrals-of-motion clustering approaches is still a matter of debate, with several
studies showing conflicting results \citep{JeanBaptiste2017,Mori2024}. 

\subsection{Stellar ages}

The lookback time since star formation of a stellar population traces 
the star formation history of its parent galaxy. Ages can be derived from directly by measuring 
oscillation frequencies in stellar interiors through asteroseismology \citep{Montalban2021}, or by
comparing photometric measurements of stars to theoretical models of stellar evolution,
either by fitting the colour--magnitude diagram of the observed stellar populations to synthetic 
isochrone models \citep{Gallart2019,Gallart2024}, or by isochrone fitting to 
individual stars \citep{Woody2025}. 

However, reliable age estimates are restricted to a narrow evolutionary window near the
main-sequence turn-off, where isochrones are well separated in the CMD \citep{Woody2025}. 
Even within this window, the need for precise parallaxes from \textit{Gaia} confines complete CMD samples to volumes of only
a few kiloparsecs \citep{Gallart2019,Woody2025}. Asteroseismology measurements, despite their greater precision, 
have so far been limited to at most a handful of halo stars \citep{Montalban2021}, although forthcoming missions
and surveys, such as PLATO \citep{Miglio2017}, will deliver asteroseismic ages for orders of magnitude more
stars.

\subsection{Contamination from the in-situ halo}

While these techniques provide a perspective into a galaxy's assembly history, they rely on being applied to 
clean samples of accreted stars, which are typically selected by applying hard cuts in chemical or kinematic space 
aimed at isolating stars in the stellar halo. While this component is the result of a galaxy's turbulent past, it does
not comprise exclusively of accreted debris. Observations \citep{Carollo2007,Carollo2010,Beers2012} and simulations \citep{Zolotov2009,Cooper2015} 
reveal a ``dual'' nature of the stellar halo with both an accreted and an in-situ origin. Several distinct mechanisms 
can cause the presence of in-situ stars in the stellar halo, such as massive mergers dynamically heating disc stars
onto halo-like orbits \citep{Belokurov2020,Mackereth2019a}, or the formation of stars ``in-situ''
from accreted or cooling gas from infalling satellites \citep{Zolotov2009,Font2011,Cooper2015}. 


The existence of an in-situ halo represents a central complication since it implies that 
the stellar halo is not a pure accretion relic and cannot be directly studied to infer a galaxy's assembly history. 
Thus, the traditional approach of applying hard cuts to isolate the accreted stars from the in-situ background as the 
two populations differ statistically: the in-situ halo is more centrally concentrated, 
tends to be in co-rotation with the disc and is on average more metal-rich, whereas the accreted component tends to
be more dynamically hot, with more randomly oriented orbits and lower metallicities \citep{Carollo2007}. However, these differences 
are not absolute, and the two populations overlap in chemo-dynamical space, making it impossible to isolate a 
pure sample of accreted stars with hard cuts.

\section{The assembly history of the Milky Way}
\label{sec:intro_mwhistory}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------


The Milky Way has been for a long time the only galaxy for which a detailed reconstruction of its assembly history has been possible \citep{Helmi2020,Deason2024}.
This is due to the high accuracy of individual star measurements that the aforementioned techniques require to infer the properties of past mergers \citep{Helmi2020}. 
In particular, the last decade has witnessed a real data revolution which allowed to map the properties of our galaxy 
and reconstruct its assembly history to an unprecedented level of detail. This has 
mostly been possible through the \textit{Gaia} mission \citep{GaiaCollaboration2016a,GaiaCollaboration2018}, which has provided accurate astrometry 
and photometry for over a billion stars, and large ground-based spectroscopic surveys, such as APOGEE \citep{Majewski2017}, GALAH \citep{Buder2021}, LAMOST \citep{Cui2012}, 
H3 \citep{Conroy2019} and \textit{Gaia}-ESO \citep{Gilmore2012}, which have provided complementary chemical abundances 
and radial velocities for millions of stars in our galaxy.

According to our current understanding, the Milky Way is a typical Sbc galaxy \citep{Gerhard2002} embedded in a dark matter 
halo of $\sim 10^{12}\,M_\odot$ \citep{Posti2019,Watkins2019,Cautun2020} in mass and $\sim290~\mathrm{kpc}$ in size \citep{Deason2020}.
On a cosmological perspective, the Galaxy is a relatively isolated system, with its nearest massive neighbour, 
the M31 galaxy, at a distance of $\sim 800$~kpc \citep{Preston2019}. Together, the Milky Way and M31 dominate the Local Group, 
a galaxy group of over a hundred observed members \citep{Karachentsev2013} that is part of the Virgo supercluster of galaxies. 
The majority of the stellar content in the Milky Way lies within a disk structure with an estimated mass of 
$3-5 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$ \citep{Licquia2016,Binney2024} and a scale length of $\sim 2.6$~kpc \citep{Licquia2016}. This component
presents two stellar populations with a high and low $[\alpha/Fe]$ abundance, namely the thick and thin disks 
\citep{Bensby2015,Nidever2014,Hayden2015,Kawata2016}. A similar distinction
can also be observed in the structural properties of the disk, with the ``geometrically-defined'' thick disk having 
a larger scale height than the thin disk \citep{Yoshii1982,Gilmore1983,Kawata2016}. The presence of a dichotomy in the disk strcuture can be 
interpreted as an evidence of an early major merger event with a gas-rich satellite, followed by a relatively quiet
phase which allowed the formation of a geometrically thin disk \citep{Brook2004,Grand2020}. 

However, the discovery of the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy and its tidal tails \citep{Ibata1994} shows that the Milky Way continues 
to cannibalise its satellites at the present day. More signatures of recent accretion events have been uncovered in the stellar halo as spatially coherent 
stellar streams, with over a hundred such structures identified through Gaia data in the inner halo \citep{Bonaca2025} and many more expected to be 
discovered in future missions such as the Vera Rubin Observatory’s Large Survey of Space and Time \citep{Ivezic2008} and the Roman Space Telescope 
\citep{Sanderson2019}.

Signatures of more ancient accretion events in the Milky Way, whose debris has long since dispersed in configuration space, 
cannot be observed directly and require a subtler approach involving the analysis of the chemodynamical properties of the accreted stars. 
The first inference of an accretion event from phase-mixed debris came with the identification of the Helmi streams \citep{Helmi1999b}, 
recognised as a coherent substructure in the velocity space of solar-neighbourhood stars. Following a similar methodolgy, more accreted substructures 
were then discovered in the inner halo after the second data release of Gaia \citep[DR2,][]{GaiaCollaboration2018}. These efforts in reconstructing
and characterising the series of mergers leading to the present-day structure of the Milky Way is commonly referred to as ``Galactic Archaeology'',
as it involves the analysis of accreted stars as ``fossils'' of the disrupted satellite galaxies where they formed \citep{Helmi2020}.
Its most significant result for Galactic Archaeology was the identification of a large population of stars on highly radial, 
eccentric orbits constituting the debris of a single massive dwarf galaxy named as Gaia--Enceladus--Sausage (GES; \citealt{Belokurov2018,Helmi2018}). 
GES is generally regarded as the last significant merger experienced by the Galaxy and is estimated to have been accreted some $8$--$10$~Gyr ago. 
The accretion of GES is thought to have left a profound imprint on the proto-Galaxy  dynamically heating pre-existing disc stars onto halo-like orbits, 
the ``Splash'' \citep{Belokurov2020}, and brought in a substantial reservoir of gas that fuelled a burst of 
in-situ star formation \citep{Bignone2019,Grand2020,Ciucua2024}. 

While the debris of the GES accretion event is expected to dominate the
accreted stellar population in the halo \citep{Naidu2020},
several smaller phase-mixed substructures have also been identified by
complementing the Gaia astrometry with radial velocity
and chemical abundance measurements from spectroscopic surveys.
The application of clustering algorithms in this chemo-dynamical space
has led to a proliferation of identified substructures,
which include the retrograde Sequoia progenitor \citep{Myeong2019}, the
 low-energy retrograde structure Thamnos \citep{Koppelman2019},
the prograde LMS-1/Wukong stream \citep{Yuan2020}
alongside the Helmi streams. A further division of the retrograde halo
into multiple populations with different metallicity
has been proposed by \citet{Naidu2020}, which includes the presence of
the Arjuna and I'itoi substructures,
which are, respectively, more metal-rich and more metal-poor than the
Sequoia debris.

Eventually, the search for the progenitors of the Milky Way has extended 
into the inner regions of the Galaxy,
where the debris of the most ancient mergers are expected to orbit. By
analysing the properties of globular clusters and field stars,
several groups have postulated the existence of one or more massive,
very early mergers, such as Heracles \citep{Horta2021}
and the ``Kraken'' progenitor inferred independently from the Galactic
globular cluster population \citep{Kruijssen2019b,Kruijssen2020}.
These events would have occurred more than $10-12$ billion years ago
and contributed to the formation of the proto-Galaxy.
However, their debris are extremely phase-mixed and chemically similar
to the primordial in-situ population \citep{Belokurov2023b},
making their definitive identification an ongoing challenge. An overview
of the main accretion events experienced by the Milky Way was proposed by \citet{Kruijssen2020} 
and is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro_MW_merger_tree}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{literature/MW_merger_tree_kraken.jpeg}
    \caption{Assembly history of the Milky Way inferred from the Galactic globular 
    cluster population using the \texttt{E-Mosaics} simulations \citep{Pfeffer2018}. Figure 
    taken from \citet{Kruijssen2020}.}
    \label{fig:intro_MW_merger_tree}
\end{figure}

As the census has grown, so too has the complexity of interpreting it
as independent accretion events. For instance, some studies assign to the Sequoia debris a standalone origin
as the remnant of a disrupted satellite galaxy \citep{Myeong2019,Matsuno2019,GarciaBethencourt2023,Ceccarelli2024},
while others argue for a connection with the GES merger, either as a group of its stars on high-energy,
retrograde orbits \citep{Koppelman2019,Koppelman2020,Amarante2022} or as a former satellite of
this system \citep{Naidu2021}.  Moreover, in many cases high-resolution chemical abundance analysis show 
that stellar debris from several accreted substructures are indistinguishable from the in-situ population 
or from the GES debris \citep{Horta2023,Ceccarelli2025}.

I argue that these complications arise because of the methodology involved in identifying these systems:
i) the separation of accreted stars from the in-situ background using hard selection cuts in the chemodynamical
space; ii) the out-of-the-box application of clustering algorithms to group these stars into their progenitors of
origin. As previously discussed, accreted and in-situ stars can present an overlap in their chemodynamical 
properties which simple analytical criteria cannot account for. This can cause clustering algorithms to 
identify spurious, heavily-contaminated substructures. Furthermore, the debris of a single progenitor can occupy a vast region of the chemo-dynamical space
\citep{JeanBaptiste2017,Mori2024}, meaning that debris from genuine progenitors can be
fragmented into multiple clusters \citep{Thomas2025}.

Addressing these limitations and evaluating their impact on observational studies requires a controlled
setting in which the true origin of every star is known,
so that the techniques used to identify and characterise the disrupted progenitors of the Galaxy can be tested,
and improved upon, against an objective ground truth.

\section{Data-driven Galactic Archaeology}
\label{sec:intro_ml}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations of Milky Way-mass haloes provide precisely such a controlled setting.
State-of-the-art simulations model, self-consistently and within a cosmological context,
the most relevent physical processes involved in Galaxy Formation
from the gravitationally-driven hierarchical structure formation to the chemical enrichment 
of the interstellar medium through unresolved stellar evolution physics \citep[e.g. see review of][]{Crain2023}. 
In recent years, efforts in improving the complexity of subgrid models has led to realisations of simulations with unprecedented physical
fidelity as modern simulated Milky Way analogues reproduce a range of observed scaling relations,
including the stellar-mass--halo-mass and
mass--metallicity relations and the surface-brightness and metallicity profiles of observed stellar
haloes, as well as the chemical and geometrical bi-modality of the stellar disk
\citep{Grand2017,Hopkins2018,Monachesi2019,Font2020,Buck2020,Agertz2021}.
However, cosmological simulations remain models of reality and the properties of
their stellar content depends strictly on the choices made in the implemented subgrid physics \citep{Kim2014,Crain2015}, which can differ
significantly across simulations.

The results in this thesis leverage the information of two
different simulation suites, \texttt{ARTEMIS} \citep{Font2020} and Auriga \citep{Grand2017,Grand2024a},
which adopt different codes and galaxy formation models. Both suites comprise high-resolution (star particle mass of $\sim10^{4}~\mathrm{M}_\odot$), zoomed-in cosmological hydrodynamical simulations of Milky Way-mass haloes.
\texttt{ARTEMIS} is run with the smoothed-particle-hydrodynamics code \texttt{GADGET-3} \citep{Springel2005b} using the
galaxy formation model developed for the \texttt{EAGLE} project \citep{Schaye2015,Crain2015}, while Auriga is run 
with the moving-mesh code \texttt{AREPO} \citep{Springel2010} using its own galaxy formation model, 
built on the imprints of the ones from \citet{Vogelsberger2013} and \citet{Marinacci2014}. 
In both suites each star particle represents a single stellar population whose
elemental abundances are inherited from the gas it formed from, which is in turn enriched by the mass loss and yields
of asymptotic giant branch stars and of core-collapse and Type~Ia supernovae. Both simulations also incorporate feedback 
from stellar outflows and active galactic nuclei. The \texttt{ARTEMIS} and Auriga suites comprise 45 and 39 galaxies of 
Milky Way-like mass ($\sim0.5-1.7~10^{12}~\mathrm{M}_\odot$), respectively. An illustration of the Milky Way analogues
in the \texttt{ARTEMIS} suite is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro_artemis}. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{literature/artemis_galaxies.jpeg}
    \caption{Composite photometry of the SDSS \textit{i}-band, \textit{r}-band, and \textit{g}-band
    for the galaxies in the \texttt{ARTEMIS} simulation suite \citep{Font2020}.
    Figure taken from \citet{Font2020}.}
    \label{fig:intro_artemis}
\end{figure}

In Galactic Archaeology, simulations have the crucial advantage that the full 
assembly history is stored in their merger trees. 
This allows to know exactly whether a star particle formed in-situ or was accreted, and to define the properties of the progenitor
galaxies at the time of infall. Thus, simulations of Milky Way-like galaxies can be used as
test beds for the observational techniques used in Galactic Archaeology studies to identify and characterise 
disrupted progenitors against an objective ground truth. Furthermore, as simulations represent are
the outcome of strictly coupled galaxy and stellar evolution processes, they can be used to extract 
insights which would otherwise be analytically impossible to derive. Thus, simulated Milky Way analogues 
are ideal development environments for machine learning techniques, which offer a data-driven descriptions 
of the processes and outcomes of Galaxy Formation.

\subsection{Overview of current machine learning methods}

As simulations of Milky Way-like galaxies have become more realistic and an increasingly larger
number of high-quality data has been released by the Gaia mission and the complementary ground-based
spectroscopic surveys, machine learning techniques have gained significant traction in Galactic
Archaeology and, more broadly, in Astrophysics \citep{Fluke2020,Ting2025}. 
This methodolgical revolution has been supported outside the field from significant
advances in Computer Science \citep[see review of][]{LeCun2015} and the 
popularisation of machine learning software development frameworks \citep{Pedregosa2011,Abadi2016,Paszke2019}.
With the term ``machine learning'' I refer to those computational methods that identify patterns in data
and use this information to execute a task which had not been explicilty programmed for. As an example, 
clustering is a type of machine learning technique leveraging the underlying density of data
points to assign to each of them to a group which is not defined a priori. Similarly, artificial neural 
networks are example of machine learning models as they use data to automatically update their internal paramters to
optimise a ``loss'' function defining the underlying task.

A first application of machine learning techniques to Galactic Archaeology was the identification of accreted
stars based on their kinematic and chemical properties. For stars accreted in
recent merger events forming stellar streams, the \texttt{STREAMFINDER} algorithm \citep{Malhan2018} 
identified 87 new structures \citep{Ibata2024} by iteratively fitting orbit ``hypertubes'' on 
astrometry and proper motions from Gaia. Another algorithm developed for finding
stellar streams is \texttt{Via~Machinae} \citep{Shih2022}, which
employs conditional density estimation to flag local overdensities in an unsupervised fashion.
Machine learning methods have also been developed to separate phase-mixed accreted stars from the
in-situ background.\citet{Veljanoski2019} developed a gradient-boosted-tree classifier 
for halo stars based on astrometric and photometric data. Similarly, \citet{Ostdiek2020} trained an artificial neural network on 
the five-dimensional kinematics of a \textit{Gaia} mock catalogue built 
from the \texttt{FIRE} simulations, fine-tuning it on \textit{Gaia} data 
and thereby enabling the discovery of the Nyx substructure \citep{Necib2020a}. The chemical
abundance space was instead explored by \citet{Tronrud2022}, who developed a ``Galactic Archaeology Neural Network'' 
trained to separate accreted and in-situ star particles in the Auriga simulations.

Clustering is another example of machine learning techniques which has been extensively 
applied for Galactic Archaeology. Density-based algorithms 
such as \texttt{DBSCAN} \citep{Ester1996} and \texttt{HDBSCAN} \citep{Campello2013}, 
as well as single-linkage hierarchical methods, have been applied to the integrals of motion and 
chemical abundances of halo stars to confirm known substructures and reveal 
new ones \citep{Koppelman2019,Borsato2020,Lovdal2022,RuizLara2022a,Dodd2023}.
A different method for the same purpose was instead developed by \citet{Yuan2018}, which 
created a self-organising map representation of the inner stellar halo which was then used to
discover the LMS-1/Wukong substructure \citep{Yuan2020}.

Machine learning algorithms have also been used to characterise the properties of the disrupted
progenitor systems from their present-day debris. A precursor of these studies was the 
work from  \citet{Kruijssen2020}, who developed an artificial neural network to regress 
the orbital parameters of progenitors from the age-metallicity relation of the
globular cluster populations in the \texttt{E-MOSAICS} simulations \citep{Pfeffer2018}.
A more probabilistic description of the merger properties has been advanced using 
using simulation-based inference (SBI; \citealt{Cranmer2020}). 
SBI is a class of methods for performing Bayesian parameter inference on models which are not
analytically defined or whose likelihood is intractable. Provided the process generating the 
observable data $\mathrm{x}$ from the model parameters $\mathrm{\theta}$ can be simulated, 
SBI consists in approximating directly the likelihood $p(\mathrm{x}|\mathrm{\theta})$ or posterior $p(\mathrm{\theta}|\mathrm{x})$ 
distributions from the simulated joint distribution of data and parameters $p(\mathrm{\theta},\mathrm{x})$. 
When the data and parameter spaces are highly-dimensional, the density estimation is performed through 
generative AI techniques, most commonly normalising flows \citep{Papamakarios2017},
which parameterise a probability distribution as a sequence of parameterised invertible 
transformations applied to a simple analytically defined base distribution. The parameters of the 
transformations are computed through artificial neural netweorks trained by 
minimising the negative log-likelihood of the training data. 

SBI has been applied across Astrophysics, from cosmological inference using galaxy clusters and 
weak-lensing maps \citep{HernandezMartinez2024,Jeffrey2021} 
to the estimation of compact-object parameters from gravitational-wave data 
\citep{Green2020}. 
On a galaxy-scale context, \citet{Widmark2025} developed an SBI framework for reconstructing the 
orbits of a tidally perturbed dwarf, NGC~205, orbiting around M31. Similarly, \citet{Brooks2026}
used simulations of the Milky Way -- Large Magellanic Cloud system to constrain the magnitude of 
the reflex velocity and the mass of the two galaxies using SBI. For the specific purpose of extimating the
properties of disrupted Milky Way satellites at the time of infall, the CASBI model \citep{Viterbo2024} 
applied the SBI formalism to accurately predict the  progenitor masses from oxygen and 
iron abundances using artificial haloes built from the NIHAO dwarf galaxy simulations.

\section{Thesis outline}
\label{sec:intro_outline}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The aim of this thesis is to extend the corpus of machine learning techniques available for reconstructing the assembly history 
of the Milky Way, replacing heuristics inherited from data-scarce times with methods that are informed by, 
and validated against, cosmological simulations and modern observational datasets. 
In this thesis, I developed novel, data-driven methods for each stage of the standard Galactic Archaeology workflow, i.e. 
identifying accreted stars, grouping them based on the systems where they formed, and reconstructing the properties of those 
galaxies at the time of accretion. All the methods were developed and tested simulated Milky Way-like galaxies from the \texttt{ARTEMIS} and Auriga
suites. Each chapter in this thesis addresses the limitations of a specific stage in the standard Galactic Archaeology workflow providing an 
alternative, data-driven solution. The chapters are organised as follows:

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter2}} addresses the separation of accreted stars 
from the in-situ background. Standard approaches rely on hard selection cuts in the chemo-dynamical space, 
which cannot account for the overlap between the two populations and inevitably produce impure accreted samples. 
Using the \texttt{ARTEMIS} simulations, I develop a data-driven classifier that learns the complex accreted--in-situ 
boundary directly from the properties of the star particles. Performing an extensive comparison of
different machine learning algorithms and classification features, I develop an model returns a smaller and purer accreted sample 
than traditional selection cuts.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter3}} addresses the grouping of the accreted debris into their progenitor systems. 
Density-based clustering algorithms are usually applied out-of-the-box returning spurious or ficticious substructures in the
Milky Way stellar halo. Selecting three Auriga galaxies with markedly different assembly histories, I develop a data-driven procedure 
to optimise the parameters of the \texttt{HDBSCAN} clustering algorithm obtaining a clustering model that
returns pure clusters dominated by star particles from a single progenitor. I also quantify
how the presence of in-situ stars degrades the performance of clustering.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter4}} addresses the reconstruction of the properties of disrupted progenitors at the time of accretion
using SBI. I introduce GalactiKit, an SBI framework trained on the merger--debris pairs of the Auriga suite,
estimating the posterior distribution of the merger properties (the infall time, stellar mass, halo mass and merger mass ratio) 
from the present-day chemo-dynamical distribution of debris. GalactiKit pioneers the application of SBI techniques for the prediction
of the properties of disrupted dwarf galaxies.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter5}} is an application of GalactiKit to the accreted substructures of the Milky Way, defined
combining samples from the \textit{Gaia} and SDSS-IV/APOGEE data. In this chapter, I address an intrinsic limitation of SBI techniques,
which is the offset between the simulations used to approximate the form of the distributions and the observations used to constrain them. 
I address the discrepancy between the Auriga subgrid physics and the real chemical evolution of the Galaxy through an explicit 
calibration+compression pre-processing pipeline. The resulting inference is the most complete and self-consistent reconstruction 
of the Milky Way assembly history to date.  

Together, these chapters trace a single methodological thread, 
from the classification of individual stars to a cosmological reconstruction of the Galaxy's growth. 
In the Conclusions, I summarises the main findings of the work in this thesis, discussing possible further improvements
and looking ahead to the opportunities that forthcoming surveys will offer for reconstructing the formation of the Milky Way and the Local Group.
