\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

The work in this thesis represents an effort in advancing the methodologies for 
reconstructing the assembly history of the Milky Way by improving and, in some cases,
pioneering the application of state-of-the-art machine learning methods. With the availability
of large amount of data from modern surveys, such an effort
moves towards the direction of a fully data-driven reconstruction fo the Galaxy merger history
which can inform and constrain our theoretical understanding of galaxy formation and the 
background cosmological context.   
SIMULATIONS
Each chapter of this thesis adresses one core challenge in the process of reconstructing the
formation history of the Milky Way by applying a machine learning technique. In the following
paragraphs I summarise the main conclusions of each of these studies.

\bigskip

In Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter2} I propose a data-driven identification of accreted stars within
the current stellar content of the Galaxy, which is dominated by stars born in-situ. I showed that
the choice of machine learning algorithm is somewhat indifferent for the task and what really matters
is the choice of the parameter space in which the distinction is drawn. The full chrono-chemodynamical
information of the stellar content allows for the optimal separation between the accreted and in-situ 
populations (purity $\sim60\%$ and completeness $\sim90\%$), however, the photometric and kinematic
properties of stars seem to also provide a ground of similar quality for the separation. This is
particularly interesting as accurate chemical abundances are harder to obtain because they require
high-resolution spectroscopic surveys and accurate spectral fitting models. A caveat of these conclusions
is that they have been drawn from cosmological simulations, which do not resolve single stars but approximate
entire stellar populations as single particles. This creates artificial differences in the distributions of the
simulations and the Milky Way, e.g. stars from a single stellar populations do follow a given isochrone and hence have
different photometry and also an intrinsic variance in their chemical abundances, which are instead assumed constant in 
simulations. The best classification model developed in the analysis is shown to outperform selection-cut based methods
when applied to star particles from solar neighbourhood regions in the simulations, which shows that the application of this
methods can have an immediate impact in the identification of accreted stars in the Milky Way/ extracting more information
on the Galaxy's past from its stellar content. 

Following the identification of accreted stars, Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter3} presents the first data-driven optimisation
of the parameters of the HDBSCAN algorithm, which is traditionally used for the identification of accreted substructure 
through the clustering of accreted stars. I show that tuning the HDBSCAN parameters to maximise the DBCV metric, an intrinsic 
metric assessing the intra-cluster density of groups, leads to a configuration of the model which is also optimal
in terms of the purity and completeness of the single clusters. This is a particularly important results as currently clustering
algorithms are used out-of-the-box or with arbitrary parameter selection and have been shown to return clusters dominated by
in-situ stars or stars from multiple progenitors \citep{Thomas2025}. The developed DBCV-driven optimisation provides instead a way of tuning the parameters of these models
to maximise cluster purity without relying on the knowledge of the where the stars formed, hence making it ideal for the direct
application to observations. When applied to pure samples of accreted stars, this clustering methodology is able to retrieve
merger events up to $z\sim3$, while in the presence of in-situ stars, this threshold is moved to $z\sim1$ due to phase-mixing 
effects and the similar chemical abundances between the different building blocks of the galaxy.

In Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter4} I develop GalactiKit, a pioneering application of simulation-based inference for the prediction of the infall time,
stellar and halo mass, and halo mass merger ratio of the disrupted progenitors of the Milky Way from the chemodynamical properties
of their debris at $z=0$. This methodology leverages the information encoded in the Auriga cosmological hydrodynamical simulations to create
a data-driven mapping between galaxy and stellar properties.
I show that total energy and angular momentum of stars can constrain the
lookback infall time of satellites, a known result for satellites \citep{Rocha2012} and now extended to phase-mixed substructures.
Similarly, I show that stelalr chemical abundances are also tracers of the satellite accretion time, due to the effect that this 
time scale has on the satellite chemical evolution and hence enrichment, but also of the satellite stellar mass, as expected from
the stellar mass-halo mass relation \citep{Kirby2013}. I also show that combining the kinematic and chemical properties of debris
provides the largest amount of information in reconstructing the properties of a satellite, with the kinematic information improving
the estimate of the stellar and halo mass estimates of low-mass, high-metallicity systems.

Finally, in Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter5} I provide an application of GalactiKit to samples of accreted stars belonging to
accreted substructures identified in the Milky Way. In this work, I address the limitation of data-driven models developed 
on simulations, as they are trained on distributions which are different to the ones they are eventually applied to. I solve this
issue by statistically modelling the discrepancy in stellar properties between the Auriga simulations and the Milky Way observations
and using this to augment the training dataset with different realisations of these ``calibration noise'', and marginalising this
uncertainty using the Fishnets data-compression model. By applying this improved version of GalactiKit, I have obtained the largest,
self-consistent reconstruction of the assembly history of the Milky Way. I predict that the accreted stellar content in out Galaxy
amounts to $2.2^{+1.1}_{-0.6} \times 10^{9}~\rm{M_{\sun}}$, most of which was accreted between $z\sim2$ and $z\sim1$. The majority 
of this mass comes from the accretion of the GES and Sagittarius dwarf galaxies, contributing 0.43 and 0.30 of the total accreted
stellar content, respectively. Combining the predictions on the halo mass and halo mass merger ratio of the Milky Way progenitors, 
I estimated the growth of the Milky Way dark matter halo predicting it is not fully explained by the current census of mergers, hence
hinting to potential undetected events, or early epoch of dominated by the accretion of dark matter-only systems.  

\bigskip

While the work in this presents data-driven methodologies for the single steps involved in a Galactic Archaelology study,
a full data-driven end-to-end pipeline that reconstruct the assembly history of our Galaxy is still to be implemented.
Indeed, while providing predictions on the Milky Way assembly history, the work in Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter5} relies on
the identification of accreted substrctures determined using out-of-the-box
clustering algorithms on samples of accreted stars selected through hard selection cuts.
However, before an end-to-end pipeline can be executed, further improvements on the single steps are still required. 
The current clustering methodology developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter3} requires complete and pure samples of accreted
stars to retrieve mergers past $z\sim1$, while the classification model developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter3}, despite 
outperforming analytical selection cuts, still retrieve accreted samples with a significant in-situ contamination (0.60 purity)
at the fiducial classification threshold. Moreover, both of these models do not include a domain adaptation technique and 
making the performance on observation data uncertain. 
Therefore, a future work direction can include the exploration of the current clustering methodolgy in case where the 
contamination of in-situ stars is significant, but not dominating to probe the relationship between the 
redshift of last possible detection and the fraction of in-situ stars in the accreted sample. This would inform on 
whether better classification models are required to better probe the assembly history of the Galaxy. 
In that case, the development of better models for the classification of 
accreted and in-situ stars by providing the information on the context of the star particle, i.e. the location of a star
in the galaxy plays a role in what features are most important for its classification as in-situ. This can be achieved exploring
attention or graph-based, artificial neural network, which provide the structural information as an extra input for the classification
as an embedding. In both cases, the inclusion of domain-adaptation techniques like the inclusion of MMD loss is required to ensure
a consistent performance when applied on observational data.
